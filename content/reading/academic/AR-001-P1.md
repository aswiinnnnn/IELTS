## Stimulus

# Cooling the City

**A** On a still summer afternoon, the centre of a large city can be several degrees warmer than the farmland that surrounds it. This phenomenon, known as the urban heat island, was first described in the early nineteenth century by Luke Howard, an amateur meteorologist who recorded temperatures in and around London. Howard noticed that the city was consistently warmer at night than the countryside, and he attributed the difference to the heat released by fuel burned in homes and workshops. Modern researchers agree that human activity plays a part, but they now regard the materials from which cities are built as the more important cause.

**B** Dark surfaces such as asphalt roads and tarred roofs absorb a large proportion of the sunlight that falls on them. During the day they store this energy, and after sunset they release it slowly into the surrounding air. Concrete and brick behave in a similar way, although they reflect slightly more light. In a rural landscape, by contrast, much of the sun's energy is used to evaporate water from soil and leaves, a process that cools the ground in much the same way that sweating cools the human body. Because cities have fewer plants and less exposed soil, this natural cooling mechanism is largely absent.

**C** The consequences extend well beyond discomfort. Higher night-time temperatures prevent people's bodies from recovering after a hot day, and public health studies have linked heat islands to increased hospital admissions among elderly residents during heatwaves. Warmer cities also consume more electricity, as air-conditioning units work harder and for longer. Ironically, these units expel hot air into the street, adding further to the problem they are designed to solve.

**D** One of the simplest responses has been to change the colour of roofs. A 'cool roof' is coated with a pale, reflective material that sends much of the incoming sunlight back into the atmosphere. In trials conducted in several American cities, the surface temperature of a white roof on a sunny day was found to be up to 30°C lower than that of a conventional black roof. The benefits for the building beneath are clear: upper floors stay cooler and air-conditioning costs fall. Whether cool roofs lower the temperature of the city as a whole is harder to establish, since roofs make up only around a fifth of the surface area of a typical urban centre.

**E** Planting trees offers a different set of advantages. A mature tree shades the ground beneath it and releases water vapour through its leaves, cooling the air around it. Street trees also absorb rainwater, reducing the risk of flooding, and many residents simply find tree-lined streets more pleasant to walk along. However, trees take years to grow, require regular maintenance, and can damage pavements and underground pipes with their roots. City authorities must therefore choose species carefully, favouring those that tolerate pollution and compacted soil.

**F** In some cities, planners have gone further and redesigned entire districts with ventilation in mind. In Stuttgart, Germany, building regulations have for decades protected the hillside corridors along which cool air flows down into the city centre at night. Construction that would block these 'air paths' is restricted, and the corridors are kept largely free of tall buildings. Similar approaches have since been adopted elsewhere, though they are difficult to apply in cities that are already densely built.

**G** Researchers increasingly argue that no single measure is sufficient. Reflective roofs, green spaces and careful planning each address a different part of the problem, and their effects appear to be greatest when they are combined. What remains uncertain is how these strategies will perform as global temperatures continue to rise, and some scientists warn that measures designed for today's climate may need to be revised within a few decades.
